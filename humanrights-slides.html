What Are Human Rights?

Rights held by all human beings simply because they are human — regardless of nationality, ethnicity, gender, religion, or any other status.

Core Characteristics

Universal — they apply to every person everywhere

Inalienable — they cannot be taken away

Indivisible — civil, political, economic, social, and cultural rights are all equally important

Interdependent — the fulfilment of one right depends on the fulfilment of others

Non-discriminatory — they belong to all without distinction

Both rights and obligations — states bear the duty to respect, protect, and fulfil

The UDHR: A Common Standard

"All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights." — Article 1, UDHR (1948)

30 articles covering civil, political, economic, social, and cultural rights. Non-binding — but incorporated into binding treaties and constitutions worldwide.

Drafted by Eleanor Roosevelt's committee, with Charles Malik (Lebanon), P.C. Chang (China), and Hansa Mehta (India).

Eleanor Roosevelt with the UDHR, 1949

The International Human Rights Regime

Key Treaties: The International Bill of Rights

ICCPR (1966)
Civil and Political Rights

  • Right to life, liberty, fair trial
  • Freedom of expression, religion, assembly
  • Protection from torture and arbitrary detention

ICESCR (1966)
Economic, Social and Cultural Rights

  • Right to work, education, health
  • Right to an adequate standard of living
  • Right to participate in cultural life

Together with the UDHR, these form the "International Bill of Rights"

Monitoring & Enforcement Mechanisms

  • UN Human Rights Council — 47 elected members; Universal Periodic Review (UPR) of all states
  • Treaty Bodies — committees of experts monitoring compliance
  • Special Procedures — independent experts (Special Rapporteurs)
  • OHCHR — led by Volker Türk
  • International Criminal Court (ICC) — prosecutes individuals for genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity

The Great Debate: Universalism vs. Relativism

Universalism

Core claim: Human rights apply to all people in all cultures at all times, because they derive from our common humanity.

Key argument: The UDHR was adopted with no dissenting votes. Its principles reflect shared moral intuitions across civilisations.

Proponents: Most Western governments, Amnesty International, HRW, the UN human rights system.

Cultural Relativism

Core claim: Rights are culturally constructed. Imposing Western-defined rights on non-Western cultures is itself a form of imperialism.

Key argument: The UDHR was drafted primarily by Western states. Many Global South nations were still colonised in 1948. The "Asian values" debate (Lee Kuan Yew, Mahathir) challenged the priority given to individual rights over collective development.

Critical question: Who defines "universal" — and who benefits from that definition?

Beyond the Binary

Most scholars reject a simple either/or:

  • Overlapping consensus — different cultures arrive at similar norms through different routes (Rawls, Sen)
  • "Thin" universalism — agreement on a minimal core (right to life, freedom from torture) with cultural variation on the margins
  • Postcolonial critique — accepting universality while demanding decolonised interpretation and implementation

Baylis, Smith & Owens, pp. 504–509

Three Generations of Rights

     

The Three Generations (Karel Vasak, 1977)

1st Generation
Civil & Political

Liberty, fair trial, free speech, vote, freedom from torture

ICCPR · "Freedom from"

2nd Generation
Economic, Social, Cultural

Work, education, health, housing, food, social security

ICESCR · "Freedom to"

3rd Generation
Solidarity / Collective

Self-determination, development, environment, peace

Emerging norms · "Freedom through"

Why Does This Framework Matter?

  • Cold War: the West prioritised 1st-generation rights; the Soviet bloc prioritised 2nd-generation rights
  • The Global South has championed 3rd-generation rights — self-determination, development
  • The indivisibility principle insists all three are equally important — but states choose which to emphasise
  • 2026: Iran's civilians suffer violations of all three simultaneously — political repression (1st), economic devastation (2nd), destruction of self-determination (3rd)

The Responsibility to Protect (R2P)

From sovereignty as a privilege to sovereignty as a responsibility

Origins

  • 1994 Rwanda: 800,000 Tutsis killed in ~100 days. The world did nothing.
  • 1999 Kosovo: NATO intervened without SC authorisation. Legal? Legitimate?
  • 2001: ICISS coins "Responsibility to Protect"
  • 2005 World Summit: 170+ heads of state unanimously adopt R2P

The Three Pillars of R2P

Pillar I

Each state must protect its population from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity.

Pillar II

The international community should assist states in building protection capacity.

Pillar III

When a state manifestly fails, the international community must take collective action — through the Security Council.

R2P applies ONLY to four crimes: genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, crimes against humanity.

Supplemental Reading #1

UN Secretary-General (May 2025)

"R2P: 20 Years of Commitment"

17th SG report · A/79/875

Key Finding: A Growing Gap

"Despite the unanimous commitment to end atrocity crimes, such crimes have increased around the world, exposing a persistent gap between promises and meaningful action."
— Secretary-General, 2025
  • Violence against civilians at highest level since 2015
  • Displaced people: 37 million (2005) → 123 million (2024)
  • More conflicts characterised by atrocity crimes than at any time since WWII

What Has Worked (Pillars I & II)

  • 12+ countries created national atrocity-prevention mechanisms
  • 118 countries have national human rights institutions
  • Regional organisations (AU, OAS, OSCE) using mediation and early warning
  • Transitional justice in Colombia, Nepal, Philippines, Timor-Leste
  • Group of Friends of R2P promotes the norm at the UN

What Has Failed (Pillar III)

  • SC paralysis: Vetoes block action on Syria, Gaza, Sudan, Myanmar, Iran
  • Double standards: R2P seen as a tool of Western intervention
  • Counter-terrorism weaponised: States use it to justify abuses
  • Attacks on aid workers: 67,414 civilian sites struck in Iran (Feb–Mar 2026)

Discussion: The SG's recommendations focus on Pillars I and II. Is this an implicit admission that Pillar III is politically dead?

The SC Deadlock: 14–1

  • 14 in favour, 1 veto (US) — a recurring pattern
  • The GA, regional bodies, and individual states must also act
  • Calls for "Uniting for Peace" (GA Res 377) to establish a protection force

Key debate: If the SC is permanently blocked by a P5 veto, does R2P have any meaning — or is it a "paper norm"?

A live test of the norm

Was R2P Invoked?

Short answer: No. The US justified strikes under Article 51 (self-defence), not R2P. Goals were strategic — nuclear programme, military leadership — not civilian protection.

However, Iran's January massacre of 30,000+ protesters should have triggered R2P. No R2P resolution was tabled because no P5 member would support it.

The Misuse Debate

"R2P did not create a loophole in international law. It was designed to ensure restraint, legitimacy, and accountability."
— Global Centre for R2P, March 2026
  • A NYT essay linked R2P to the Iran war. The Global Centre rejected this
  • R2P covers only four crimes; it does not authorise unilateral strikes
  • The real problem: R2P is being ignored, not abused

Human Rights Violations in the Iran War

  • US/Israel: 498 schools, 236 health facilities struck; Minab school (~175 dead); threats to energy, water, nuclear infrastructure
  • Hegseth's "no quarter": A war crime under the Hague Convention and US law (18 USC §2441)
  • Civilian protection dismantled: DoD removed protection teams, replaced JAGs, omitted international law from defence strategy
  • Iran: Strikes on civilian infrastructure across Gulf states — cities, hotels, airports

Sources: Just Security (Apr 2, 2026); OHCHR (Mar 4, 2026)

Q2: Should R2P have been invoked for Iran's massacre of 30,000+ protesters — before the war?

Q3: If R2P can't be applied to Palestine or Iran because the perpetrator (or ally) holds a veto, what reforms would work?

Enforcement & Accountability

The International Criminal Court

  • Rome Statute (2002); 124 member states
  • Jurisdiction: genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity, aggression
  • Key limitation: US, Russia, China, India, Israel, Iran are NOT members
  • 2024: ICC arrest warrant for Netanyahu — landmark
  • 2026: Calls for Iran to accept ICC jurisdiction over US strikes
  • 2026: US sanctions on ICC intensified

Beyond the ICC

  • ICJ: South Africa's genocide case against Israel (ongoing)
  • Universal Jurisdiction: Germany, Spain prosecute regardless of location
  • Commissions of Inquiry: UN fact-finding on Syria, Gaza, Sudan
  • Sanctions: Travel bans, asset freezes on individuals
  • Civil society: DAWN, Al-Haq, Amnesty, HRW preserve evidence

The Accountability Gap

Core problem: The most powerful states evade accountability. The US is not an ICC member, uses its veto to shield allies, and sanctions ICC officials.

Result: The ICC has overwhelmingly prosecuted African leaders — fuelling charges of selective justice and neo-colonialism.

Human Rights and Cyprus

Where you sit matters — these concepts are not abstract here

The Missing Persons: A 50-Year Wound

The Committee on Missing Persons (CMP) works to recover 2,002 people who disappeared in 1963–64 and 1974.

  • Excavations ongoing as of March 2026
  • Families have waited 50+ years
  • SC Res 2815 (2026) calls for continued support
  • This is a right to truth issue

Discussion: The right to know the fate of a missing relative is increasingly recognised as a human right. How does this connect to the Convention on Enforced Disappearance (2006)?

Press Freedom and Civic Space in the North

The 2025 OHCHR report (A/HRC/58/21) documented:

  • Şener Levent — convicted in absentia by Ankara for criticising Turkey. 6-month sentence.
  • Ali Kişmir — facing "insulting security forces" charges. Potential 10-year sentence. Postponed 9 times.
  • Plainclothes police monitored civil society events — "chilling effect."

These are 1st-generation rights violations happening within kilometres of this classroom.

Migrant Workers and Exploitation

  • Sept 2025: UN experts condemned exploitation at a citrus farm in the north — threats, coercion, violence
  • 2026 Refugee Law criticised for allowing child detention
  • National Migrant Integration Strategy 2026–2029 adopted — implementation contested
  • CERD reviewing Cyprus's record, April–May 2026

Question: How do 2nd-generation rights (work, fair conditions) intersect with 1st-generation rights (freedom from forced labour) in migrant workers' lives on this island?

The 2026 Iran War Comes to Cyprus

  • 1 March: Hezbollah drone struck RAF Akrotiri — first attack since 1974
  • European navies mobilised (Greece, France, Italy, Germany, Netherlands, Spain)
  • Turkey sent F-16s to Northern Cyprus — Greek-Turkish tensions spiked

Q: Iran's IRGC general threatened to "launch missiles at Cyprus." How does this relate to 3rd-generation rights — the right to peace?

UNFICYP condemned the act: "Acts of violence of this nature are unacceptable and risk undermining public safety and stability."

Key Takeaways

What to Remember

  1. Human rights are universal, indivisible, interdependent — in theory. In practice, enforcement is selective.
  2. The regime (UDHR → ICCPR/ICESCR → ICC) created norms that matter — but lack enforcement against major states.
  3. R2P: 20 years later, a "persistent gap between promises and action."
  4. The P5 veto is the single biggest structural obstacle.
  5. The 2026 Iran war is a live laboratory for every concept in this lecture.
  6. Human rights are not abstract — you are sitting in a divided capital, metres from a buffer zone, in a country hit by a drone from a war 2,000 km away.
