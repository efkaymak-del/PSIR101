What Are Human Rights?

⚖️

Rights held by all human beings simply because they are human — regardless of nationality, ethnicity, gender, religion, or any other status.

Core Characteristics

Universal — they apply to every person everywhere

Inalienable — they cannot be taken away

Indivisible — civil, political, economic, social, and cultural rights are all equally important

Interdependent — the fulfilment of one right depends on the fulfilment of others

Non-discriminatory — they belong to all without distinction

Both rights and obligations — states bear the duty to respect, protect, and fulfil

The UDHR: A Common Standard

"All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights." — Article 1, UDHR (1948)

30 articles covering civil, political, economic, social, and cultural rights. Non-binding declaration — but its principles have been incorporated into binding treaties and domestic constitutions worldwide.

Drafted by a committee chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt, with contributions from across cultures — including Lebanese philosopher Charles Malik, Chinese diplomat P.C. Chang, and Indian feminist Hansa Mehta.

The International Human Rights Regime

🌐

Key Treaties: The International Bill of Rights

ICCPR (1966)
International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights

  • Right to life, liberty, fair trial
  • Freedom of expression, religion, assembly
  • Protection from torture and arbitrary detention
  • Monitored by the Human Rights Committee

ICESCR (1966)
International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights

  • Right to work, education, health
  • Right to an adequate standard of living
  • Right to participate in cultural life
  • Monitored by CESCR

Together with the UDHR, these form the "International Bill of Rights"

Specialised Conventions

ConventionYearFocus

Monitoring & Enforcement Mechanisms

  • UN Human Rights Council — 47 elected members; Universal Periodic Review (UPR) of all states
  • Treaty Bodies — committees of experts monitoring compliance with each convention
  • Special Procedures — independent experts (Special Rapporteurs) investigating thematic or country-specific issues
  • Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR) — led by Volker Türk
  • International Criminal Court (ICC) — prosecutes individuals for genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity

The Great Debate: Universalism vs. Relativism

🤔

Universalism

Core claim: Human rights are universal — they apply to all people in all cultures at all times, because they derive from our common humanity.

Key argument: The UDHR was adopted by the General Assembly with no dissenting votes. Its principles reflect shared moral intuitions across civilisations.

Proponents: Most Western governments, major international NGOs (Amnesty, HRW), the UN human rights system itself.

Cultural Relativism

Core claim: Rights are culturally constructed. What counts as a "right" varies across societies. Imposing Western-defined rights on non-Western cultures is itself a form of imperialism.

Key argument: The UDHR was drafted primarily by Western states. Many Global South nations were still colonised in 1948. The "Asian values" debate of the 1990s (Lee Kuan Yew, Mahathir) challenged the priority given to individual civil/political rights over collective economic/social development.

Critical question: Who defines "universal" — and who benefits from that definition?

Beyond the Binary

Most scholars today reject a simple either/or. The textbook identifies several middle positions:

  • Overlapping consensus — different cultures arrive at similar norms through different philosophical routes (Rawls, Sen)
  • "Thin" universalism — agreement on a minimal core (right to life, freedom from torture) while allowing cultural variation on the margins
  • Postcolonial critique — accepting the universality of rights while demanding that their interpretation and implementation be decolonised

Baylis, Smith & Owens, pp. 504–509

Discussion Prompt

Case: In 2026, the US government described its rules of engagement as "stupid" and stated "I don't need international law" (Trump, January 2026). Secretary of Defence Hegseth declared "no quarter, no mercy." Over 100 international law professors condemned this rhetoric.

Question: When a major Western democracy — historically the loudest proponent of universal human rights — publicly dismisses international law during a war, what does this do to the universalism argument? Does it validate the cultural relativist critique, or is this a case of hypocrisy rather than relativism?

Three Generations of Rights

🏛️ → 🏥 → 🌍

The Three Generations (Karel Vasak, 1977)

1st Generation
Civil & Political

Liberty, fair trial, free speech, vote, privacy, freedom from torture

ICCPR · "Freedom from"

2nd Generation
Economic, Social, Cultural

Work, education, health, housing, food, social security

ICESCR · "Freedom to"

3rd Generation
Solidarity / Collective

Self-determination, development, healthy environment, peace

Emerging norms · "Freedom through"

Why Does This Framework Matter?

  • During the Cold War, the West prioritised 1st-generation rights (political freedoms) while the Soviet bloc prioritised 2nd-generation rights (economic equality)
  • The Global South has championed 3rd-generation rights — particularly self-determination and the right to development
  • The indivisibility principle insists all three generations are equally important — but in practice, states choose which rights to emphasise
  • 2026 relevance: Iran's civilian population is suffering violations of all three generations simultaneously — political repression (1st), economic devastation from war and sanctions (2nd), and destruction of self-determination (3rd)

The Responsibility to Protect (R2P)

🛡️

From sovereignty as a privilege to sovereignty as a responsibility

Origins

  • 1994 Rwanda: 800,000 Tutsis killed in ~100 days. The international community did nothing. Kofi Annan later asked: should a willing coalition have stood by?
  • 1999 Kosovo: NATO intervened without Security Council authorisation. Legal? Legitimate?
  • 2001: International Commission on Intervention and State Sovereignty (ICISS) coins "Responsibility to Protect"
  • 2005 World Summit: 170+ heads of state unanimously adopt R2P

The Three Pillars of R2P

Pillar I

Each state has the primary responsibility to protect its population from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, and crimes against humanity.

Pillar II

The international community should assist states in building capacity to protect their populations.

Pillar III

When a state manifestly fails to protect its population, the international community must be prepared to take timely and decisive collective action — through the Security Council.

R2P applies ONLY to four specific crimes: genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing, crimes against humanity.

📖 Supplemental Reading

UN Secretary-General (May 2025)

"Responsibility to Protect: 20 Years of Commitment to Principled and Collective Action"

17th SG report on R2P · A/79/875

Key Finding: A Growing Gap

"Despite the unanimous commitment to end atrocity crimes, such crimes have increased around the world, exposing a persistent gap between promises and meaningful action."
— Secretary-General, 2025
  • Violence against civilians at highest level since 2015
  • Forcibly displaced people: from 37 million (2005) → 123 million (2024)
  • Historic number of global conflicts characterised by atrocity crimes — the most since WWII

What Has Worked (Pillars I & II)

  • 12+ countries created national atrocity-prevention mechanisms
  • 118 countries now have national human rights institutions
  • Regional organisations (AU, OAS, OSCE) using mediation and early warning
  • Transitional justice processes in Colombia, Nepal, Philippines, Timor-Leste
  • Group of Friends of R2P promotes the norm through joint statements at the UN

What Has Failed (Pillar III)

  • Security Council paralysis: Veto use (or threat) by P5 members has blocked action on Syria, Gaza, Sudan, Myanmar, and now Iran
  • Double standards: Selective application fuels perception that R2P is a tool of Western intervention, not universal protection
  • Weaponisation of counter-terrorism: States use counter-terror justifications to commit abuses while avoiding accountability
  • Attacks on humanitarian workers: At record levels; 67,414 civilian sites struck in Iran alone (Feb–Mar 2026)

SG's Recommendations

  1. National mechanisms: Every state should establish permanent domestic atrocity-prevention structures
  2. Regional learning: Share experiences across regions; strengthen AU/OSCE/ASEAN capacities
  3. Technical guidance: Develop practical toolkits for implementing R2P at domestic, regional, and multilateral levels

Discussion: The SG's recommendations focus on Pillars I and II. Is this an implicit admission that Pillar III (collective military action) is politically dead?

Global Centre for R2P (September 2025)

"Urgent Call to Action: Responsibility to Protect the Palestinian People"

The Argument

"The genocide in Gaza lays bare the consequences of inaction and double standards in the face of crimes that shock the conscience of humanity — consequences R2P was explicitly designed to prevent."
— Global Centre for R2P, Sept 2025
  • Palestinians subjected to "unrelenting escalation of atrocity crimes" for nearly two years
  • Famine spreading; tens of thousands killed; 90% of Gaza under military zones or forced displacement orders
  • Israel's Pillar I obligation — to protect populations under its control as occupying power — manifestly failed
  • International community's Pillar III obligation triggered

States That Have Acted Under R2P

State/GroupAction

The SC Deadlock: 14–1

  • A pattern has emerged in the Security Council: 14 members in favour of action, 1 veto (US)
  • The Global Centre argues the SC is not the only body with R2P responsibilities — the GA, regional organisations, and individual states must also act
  • Calls for using "Uniting for Peace" (GA Resolution 377) to establish a multinational protection force

Key debate: If the Security Council is permanently blocked by a P5 veto, does R2P have any meaning? Or does it become, as critics allege, merely a "paper norm"?

The Global Centre's Recommendations

  • Establish a multinational protection force via GA emergency special session
  • Comprehensive arms embargo on Israel
  • Immediate, unhindered humanitarian access to Gaza
  • Full access for ICC investigators, media, and human rights monitors
  • Sanctions — political, economic, and military — on Israel
  • Ad hoc criminal accountability mechanism for crimes in OPT since 1967

Note: These are the Centre's recommendations. Students should evaluate them critically — are they politically feasible? Are they consistent with R2P's three pillars?

R2P and the 2026 Iran War

💥

A live test of the norm

Timeline Recap

DateEvent
Jan 8, 2026Iranian security forces kill 30,000+ protesters; Trump threatens intervention
Feb 28US & Israel launch Operation Epic Fury; Khamenei killed
Feb 28Shajareh Tayyebeh school in Minab struck — ~175 civilians killed, many children
Mar 2Hezbollah drone strikes RAF Akrotiri, Cyprus
Mar 11SC Resolution 2817 condemns Iran's retaliation (Russia/China abstain)
Apr 2100+ international law professors publish open letter: strikes violate UN Charter
Apr 7–8Pakistan-brokered two-week ceasefire

Was R2P Invoked?

Short answer: No. The Trump administration justified the strikes under Article 51 (self-defence), not R2P. The stated goals were strategic — targeting Iran's nuclear programme and military leadership — not civilian protection.

However, some commentators argued that Iran's January 2026 massacre of 30,000+ protesters should have triggered R2P. But R2P requires action through the Security Council, and no R2P resolution was tabled.

The Misuse Debate

"R2P did not create a loophole in international law. On the contrary, it was designed to ensure restraint, legitimacy, and accountability."
— Global Centre for R2P, March 2026
  • A New York Times essay tried to link R2P to the Iran war. The Global Centre firmly rejected this
  • R2P applies only to four specific crimes; it does not authorise unilateral military action for strategic purposes
  • The real problem: not that R2P is being abused, but that it is being ignored — in Palestine, Sudan, Myanmar, and Iran itself

Human Rights Violations in the Iran War

  • Jus in bello violations (US/Israel): 498 schools, 236 health facilities struck; Minab school attack (~175 dead, many children); threats to energy infrastructure, desalination plants, nuclear facilities
  • Hegseth's "no quarter" statement: Declaring "no quarter" is a war crime under the Hague Convention and US law (18 USC §2441)
  • Civilian harm infrastructure dismantled: DoD removed civilian protection teams, replaced Judge Advocates General, omitted international law from 2026 National Defence Strategy
  • Iran's violations: Strikes on civilian infrastructure across Gulf states; retaliatory attacks on cities, hotels, airports in UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar

Sources: Just Security (Apr 2, 2026), 100+ professors' open letter; OHCHR (Mar 4, 2026)

Discussion

Q1: The 2026 Iran war saw one of the deadliest US strikes on civilians in decades (Minab school). Trump claimed Iran was responsible; the DoD's own investigation found the US conducted the strike. How does this affect the credibility of the US as a human rights advocate?

Q2: Iran's January 2026 massacre of protesters killed 30,000+. Should R2P have been invoked then — before the war? What prevented it?

Q3: If R2P cannot be applied to either Palestine or Iran because the perpetrator (or its ally) holds a P5 veto, what reforms would make R2P functional?

Enforcement & Accountability

⚖️

The International Criminal Court

  • Established by the Rome Statute (2002); 124 member states
  • Jurisdiction over genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity, aggression
  • Key limitation: US, Russia, China, India, Israel, Iran are NOT members
  • 2024: ICC issued arrest warrant for Israeli PM Netanyahu — a landmark decision
  • 2026: Calls for Iran to accept ICC jurisdiction over US/Israeli strikes on Iranian soil
  • 2026: US sanctions on ICC intensified under Trump administration

Beyond the ICC: Other Accountability Mechanisms

  • International Court of Justice (ICJ): South Africa's genocide case against Israel (ongoing since Dec 2023)
  • Universal Jurisdiction: Some states (Germany, Spain) prosecute international crimes regardless of where they occurred
  • Commissions of Inquiry: UN-mandated fact-finding bodies (e.g., on Syria, Gaza, Sudan)
  • Sanctions: Targeted measures against individuals and entities (travel bans, asset freezes)
  • Civil society documentation: Organisations like DAWN, Al-Haq, Amnesty, HRW preserve evidence for future prosecutions

The Accountability Gap

Core problem: The most powerful states can evade accountability. The US is not an ICC member; it uses its P5 veto to shield allies from SC referrals; and it actively sanctions ICC officials who investigate its allies.

Result: The ICC has overwhelmingly prosecuted African leaders — fuelling charges of selective justice and neo-colonialism. Until major powers are held to the same standards, the human rights regime will lack legitimacy in the eyes of much of the world.

IR Theories on Human Rights

🔍

How Each Theory Sees Human Rights

TheoryView of Human Rights
RealismStates use human rights rhetoric instrumentally to advance national interests. Power, not norms, determines outcomes. The Iran war illustrates this: the US invoked "self-defence," not human rights.
LiberalismInternational institutions and norms can constrain state behaviour. The UDHR, ICC, and R2P represent genuine progress — but require political will to enforce.
ConstructivismHuman rights norms are socially constructed and can reshape state identity and behaviour over time (the "norm cascade"). But norms can also be contested and eroded.
MarxismHuman rights mask economic exploitation. 1st-generation political rights distract from structural inequality. Real emancipation requires challenging global capitalism.
PostcolonialismThe human rights regime reflects Western power. Its selective enforcement — strong against weak states, silent against strong ones — reproduces colonial hierarchies.
FeminismHuman rights frameworks have historically privileged the public sphere (politics, law) while ignoring violations in the private sphere (domestic violence, reproductive rights).

Key Takeaways

What to Remember

  1. Human rights are universal, indivisible, and interdependent — in theory. In practice, their enforcement is selective and politicised.
  2. The international regime (UDHR → ICCPR/ICESCR → ICC) has created norms that matter — but lacks enforcement power against major states.
  3. R2P was a breakthrough in 2005. Twenty years later, the SG acknowledges a "persistent gap between promises and action."
  4. The P5 veto is the single biggest structural obstacle to human rights protection via the Security Council.
  5. The 2026 Iran war is a live laboratory for every concept in this lecture: jus ad bellum, jus in bello, R2P, ICC jurisdiction, universalism vs. selectivity, and the limits of international law when power trumps norms.
